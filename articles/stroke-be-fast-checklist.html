Recognising a Stroke Fast: The BE-FAST Checklist

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team4 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a blood vessel in the brain bleeds. Brain cells begin to die within minutes, so every minute of delay can mean more lasting damage. Knowing the warning signs, and acting without hesitation, can save a life and protect a person's independence.

Types of stroke

An ischaemic stroke, the more common kind, is caused by a clot or blockage. A haemorrhagic stroke is caused by bleeding in or around the brain. A transient ischaemic attack (TIA), sometimes called a mini-stroke, causes stroke symptoms that pass within minutes or hours. A TIA is still an emergency, because it often warns that a full stroke may follow.

The BE-FAST checklist

BE-FAST is an easy way to remember the signs. Look for any one of them, appearing suddenly:

  • B, Balance: sudden loss of balance, dizziness or trouble walking.
  • E, Eyes: sudden blurred, double or lost vision in one or both eyes.
  • F, Face: one side of the face droops or feels numb. Ask the person to smile and see if it is uneven.
  • A, Arm: weakness or numbness in one arm or leg. Ask the person to raise both arms and see if one drifts down.
  • S, Speech: slurred, confused or strange speech, or trouble understanding you.
  • T, Time: note exactly when symptoms began, and get emergency help at once.

Other signs can include a sudden, severe headache unlike any before, sudden confusion, or difficulty swallowing.

What to do right away

  1. Call for emergency help immediately. Use your local emergency number, or get the person to the nearest hospital that can treat emergencies. Do not wait to see if the symptoms pass.
  2. Note the time. Write down when the symptoms began, or when the person was last seen well. Doctors use this to decide which treatments are possible. Some treatments only work within a limited window.
  3. Keep the person safe and comfortable. Help them sit or lie down, loosen tight clothing, and stay with them. If they become unconscious but are breathing, place them on their side.
  4. Give nothing by mouth. No food, drink or tablets, because swallowing may be affected and they could choke. Do not give aspirin or other medicines unless the emergency team tells you to, since some strokes involve bleeding and aspirin can make that worse.
  5. Tell the team what you know. Share the person's medicines, medical conditions such as high blood pressure or diabetes, and what happened.

Do not wait it out

A common and dangerous mistake is waiting for symptoms to improve, or waiting until morning. Even if the signs fade, get checked. A TIA can be followed by a major stroke within hours or days, and treatment after a TIA can prevent it.

Who is at risk

High blood pressure is the biggest controllable risk factor for stroke. Other risks include diabetes, smoking, heavy alcohol use, high cholesterol, an irregular heartbeat, being overweight, little physical activity and a family history of stroke. Risk rises with age, but strokes can affect younger adults too.

Prevention

Much stroke can be prevented. Check your blood pressure regularly and take prescribed medicine consistently. Eat less salt, stay active, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and manage diabetes carefully. See our article on blood pressure stages to understand your readings, and the first 60 seconds of a health emergency for what to do in other urgent situations.

Life after a stroke

Recovery varies widely. Rehabilitation, with physiotherapy, speech therapy and family support, can help many people regain function. Caregivers should look after their own health too. Reducing the chance of a second stroke depends on the same steps as prevention.

The most important thing to remember is simple: if you see one of these signs, do not wait. Act fast.

Key takeaways

  • BE-FAST stands for Balance, Eyes, Face, Arm, Speech, Time.
  • Note when symptoms began and call emergency services at once.
  • Do not give food, drink or tablets by mouth.

Quick FAQ

What if the symptoms go away on their own?

Brief stroke-like symptoms can still be a warning. Get medical help straight away anyway.
