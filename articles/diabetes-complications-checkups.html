Diabetes Complications: Why Regular Check-ups Matter Even When You Feel Fine

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team4 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

Over many years, blood sugar that stays above target can harm small and large blood vessels and the nerves. Because the early changes are usually painless, people often feel completely well while damage quietly develops. This is the main reason doctors schedule regular check-ups for people with diabetes even when they have no complaints. This article explains what is checked, why, and how often to ask about it.

Eyes

High blood sugar can damage the tiny vessels in the retina, a condition called diabetic retinopathy, which is a leading cause of vision loss. It is far easier to treat when found early. Diabetes can also raise the risk of cataracts and glaucoma. Many people with diabetes need a dilated eye examination at least once a year, but your doctor will advise the right interval for you. Seek care quickly for sudden changes in vision, new floaters or flashes of light.

Kidneys

The kidneys filter blood through very small vessels that diabetes can damage. Early kidney disease usually causes no symptoms, so it is found with simple tests: a urine test for protein and a blood test that shows how well the kidneys are filtering. These are generally done at least once a year. Controlling blood sugar and blood pressure protects the kidneys. Swelling in the feet or face, unusual tiredness and foamy urine should be mentioned to your doctor.

Nerves and feet

Nerve damage can cause numbness, tingling or burning, usually starting in the feet. It can also reduce feeling, so small cuts or blisters go unnoticed and may become serious infections. Poor blood flow slows healing even more. This is why foot care matters so much:

  • Look at your feet every day, including between the toes. Use a mirror or ask someone to help.
  • Wash and dry them carefully, and moisturise dry skin but not between the toes.
  • Wear well-fitting shoes and clean socks, and check inside shoes for stones before putting them on.
  • Never walk barefoot where there may be sharp objects, including at home.
  • Do not cut corns or calluses yourself. Ask a health professional.

Ask your doctor or nurse to examine your feet at least once a year, and at every visit if you have numbness or a past foot problem. Seek care promptly for a wound that is slow to heal, redness, swelling, a bad smell or any change in skin colour.

Heart and blood vessels

Diabetes raises the risk of heart disease and stroke. Blood pressure and cholesterol matter as much as blood sugar. Have your blood pressure checked at each visit and cholesterol tested as your doctor advises. See our articles on blood pressure stages and the warning signs of stroke.

Long-term sugar control

A blood test often called HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over the past two to three months. Many people have it checked every three to six months. Ask your doctor what your own target is, since it can differ by age and health.

Teeth and gums

People with diabetes are more prone to gum disease and infections. Brush twice a day, and see a dentist regularly. Tell the dentist you have diabetes.

What you can do between visits

  • Take medicines as prescribed and keep your appointments.
  • Keep a log of blood sugar readings and bring it to visits.
  • Follow a steady eating pattern, and stay active. See our daily diabetes routine.
  • Do not smoke, and limit alcohol.

Questions to ask at your next visit

  • When was my last eye, kidney and foot check, and when is the next?
  • What is my target for blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol?
  • Which warning signs should make me contact you early?

Regular check-ups let problems be found while they are still small and treatable. Feeling well is a good sign, but it is not a reason to skip them.

Key takeaways

  • Early diabetes damage is usually painless, so check-ups matter.
  • Eyes, kidneys, feet and heart need regular review.
  • Check your feet daily and report wounds that heal slowly.

Quick FAQ

How often should I have my eyes checked?

Many people with diabetes need an eye examination at least once a year, but your doctor will advise the right interval for you.
