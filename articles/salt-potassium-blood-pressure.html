Salt, Potassium and Blood Pressure: Small Changes That Add Up

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team3 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

Salt and blood pressure are closely linked. Sodium, the main part of salt, makes the body hold on to water. This increases the volume of blood in your vessels and the pressure on their walls. Cutting back on sodium, and eating more foods rich in potassium, are two of the most effective food changes for lowering blood pressure.

How much salt is too much?

The World Health Organization advises adults to eat less than about 5 grams of salt a day, roughly one level teaspoon in total from all sources. Many people eat much more without realising it.

Where the sodium really comes from

Most of the sodium people eat does not come from the salt shaker. It is hidden in other foods:

  • Seasoning cubes and powders, which many recipes use two or three times in one pot.
  • Processed meats such as sausages, corned beef and suya spice mixes.
  • Instant noodles, packaged snacks and canned foods.
  • Salted fish, stockfish and crayfish used for flavour.
  • Bread, pastries and restaurant or street food.

Reading labels and cooking from fresh ingredients more often is usually more effective than simply removing the shaker from the table. Compare the sodium on labels and choose the lower option.

How to cut back without losing flavour

  • Flavour food with onions, garlic, ginger, pepper, scent leaf, thyme, curry, lemon and other herbs.
  • Use one seasoning cube instead of two, then try half a cube, and so on. Reduce gradually.
  • Taste before adding salt, and add it at the end of cooking, not the start.
  • Rinse canned beans and vegetables, and go easy on stock and salted fish.
  • Ask for less salt and seasoning when eating out, and avoid adding extra salt to your plate.

Taste buds adjust within a few weeks. Many people find that food they once enjoyed starts to taste too salty. For recipes and ideas, read cooking low-salt Nigerian meals.

What potassium does

Potassium works in the opposite direction. It helps the kidneys remove sodium and helps relax the walls of blood vessels. Good sources include:

  • Beans and lentils.
  • Leafy vegetables such as spinach and ugu.
  • Tomatoes, bananas, oranges and pawpaw.
  • Sweet potatoes and yam, and other vegetables and fruit.

Eating a variety of vegetables, fruit, beans and whole foods, and fewer processed ones, naturally lowers sodium and raises potassium.

When to be careful

Potassium is not safe for everyone in large amounts. People with kidney disease, and people who take certain blood pressure medicines such as some ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics, can have dangerously high potassium. Ask your doctor before changing your intake substantially, and before using any salt substitute. Many of these products are made with potassium.

Other helpful habits

Salt is only one piece. Staying active, keeping a healthy weight, limiting alcohol, not smoking, sleeping well and taking prescribed medicine all matter. Salt reduction also helps your medicine work better. Track your progress with a home log, as explained in how to measure at home, and read blood pressure stages to understand your numbers.

Start with one change this week, such as halving the seasoning cubes in your main pot. Small changes add up.

Key takeaways

  • Most dietary sodium comes from seasoning cubes, processed and canned foods, not just the salt shaker.
  • Foods such as beans, leafy vegetables, tomatoes and bananas supply potassium.
  • Taste buds adjust to less salt within a few weeks.

Quick FAQ

Can I use salt substitutes?

Many contain potassium, which is unsafe for some people with kidney disease or on certain medicines. Ask your doctor first.
