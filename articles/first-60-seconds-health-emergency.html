What to Actually Do in the First 60 Seconds of a Health Emergency

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team4 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

In a real emergency, the first minute is almost never spent on treatment. It is spent on communication: working out who to call, finding the number, explaining where you are, and hoping someone answers. That minute is where most time is lost, and much of it can be saved with a little preparation done calmly, in advance. This article covers what to have ready and what to do in the first moments.

Get these things ready before you need them

  • A named emergency contact. Decide who your primary contact is, and who is the backup. Do not rely on "whoever picks up first".
  • Basic medical information. Write down your blood type if you know it, allergies, long-term conditions, current medicines and doses, and your doctor's number. Keep it where a family member can reach it without unlocking your phone.
  • Emergency numbers. Nigeria has a national emergency number, 112, but coverage varies by area. Also save the direct numbers of the nearest hospital and any ambulance service, and put them where the family can see them.
  • Your address and landmarks. Be able to describe where you live clearly, because responders often rely on landmarks.

The first 60 seconds

  1. Check it is safe. Look for dangers such as traffic, fire, fallen wires or an unstable building. Do not put yourself at risk.
  2. Call for help early. Do not delay the call to try treatment first. If someone else is with you, ask them to call while you stay with the person.
  3. Say the essentials. Give your exact location, what happened, how many people are hurt, whether the person is awake and breathing, and a number they can call back on. Stay on the line until told to hang up.
  4. Note the time. Write down when symptoms began. Responders use this to decide on treatment.

Check responsiveness and breathing

Tap the person's shoulders and call out loudly. If they do not respond, check whether they are breathing normally. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing. If they are unresponsive and not breathing normally, shout for help, call emergency services, and start hands-only CPR: place the heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, put your other hand on top, and push hard and fast, about 100 to 120 pushes a minute, letting the chest rise fully between pushes. Continue until help arrives or the person starts to breathe. A trained responder may add breaths, but pushing on the chest alone is far better than doing nothing.

Other urgent situations

  • Severe bleeding: press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth and keep pressing. Do not remove the cloth if blood soaks through. Add more on top.
  • Suspected stroke: use the BE-FAST checklist in our stroke article, and get help immediately.
  • Chest pain or trouble breathing: call for help at once and keep the person resting and calm.
  • Choking, burns and falls: see our guide to home first aid basics.

Do not wait to see if it passes

For sudden chest pain, difficulty breathing, signs of a stroke, severe bleeding, a seizure that does not stop, or loss of consciousness, act immediately. It is better to call and not need help than to wait and lose precious time.

Prepare the people around you

Show family members where your medical information is kept and who to call. For people managing long-term conditions such as hypertension or diabetes, the goal is not to prevent every emergency, because some cannot be prevented. The goal is to make sure the response takes seconds instead of minutes. Tools such as an emergency SOS feature that alerts saved contacts can help, but they work best alongside a written plan the whole household knows.

Take ten minutes this week to write down your contacts and medical details, and share them with one other person. It could make all the difference.

Key takeaways

  • Check that the area is safe, then call for help before doing anything else.
  • Know your local emergency number and keep it where family can see it.
  • Stay with the person, note the time symptoms started and share it with responders.

Quick FAQ

Should I wait to see if symptoms pass?

For sudden chest pain, trouble breathing, stroke signs, severe bleeding or loss of consciousness, no. Get help immediately.
