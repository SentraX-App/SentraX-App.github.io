Living With Diabetes: Building a Routine You Can Actually Keep

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team4 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

Managing diabetes is mostly about steady habits rather than dramatic effort. A simple routine around food, movement, checks and medicine keeps blood sugar steadier and lowers the risk of complications. This article describes the everyday habits that help, as general information to discuss with your own healthcare team.

A quick background

Diabetes means the body either does not make enough insulin or cannot use it properly, so sugar builds up in the blood. Type 1 diabetes needs insulin. Type 2 diabetes, the more common kind, is often managed with food, activity and tablets, sometimes with insulin as well. Your doctor will tell you which applies and set your targets.

Eating regularly

Regular meals help prevent sharp swings in blood sugar. A helpful guide is to fill about half your plate with vegetables, about a quarter with protein such as fish, beans or eggs, and about a quarter with starchy food such as rice, yam, beans or swallow. Portion size matters, because starch breaks down into sugar. Choose whole and less processed foods when you can, and limit sugary drinks. Our article on a diabetes-friendly Nigerian plate gives practical examples.

Moving your body

Physical activity helps your body use insulin better. Walking for about thirty minutes on most days is a good aim, and even a ten-minute walk after a meal can help. If you use insulin or certain tablets, ask your doctor how to exercise safely, since activity can lower blood sugar.

Checking and recording

Check your blood sugar at the times your doctor recommends, and write down every result with the time and what you ate. Patterns are more useful than any single number. Take your record to every appointment. Do not change your medicine based on one reading, and tell your doctor if readings are often outside your target range.

Low blood sugar

Low blood sugar can happen with insulin or certain tablets, especially if you skip a meal or exercise more than usual. Signs include shakiness, sweating, sudden hunger, a pounding heart, dizziness and confusion. A common approach is to take about 15 grams of fast-acting sugar, such as glucose tablets or half a glass of juice or a regular soft drink, then recheck after about 15 minutes. Ask your doctor to confirm what is right for you. Keep a fast-acting sugar source within reach, and make sure family members know the signs. If a person cannot swallow safely or is unconscious, call emergency help.

High blood sugar and sick days

Very high readings, extreme thirst, frequent urination and tiredness are warning signs. During illness, blood sugar can rise even if you eat less. Keep taking your medicine unless your doctor says otherwise, drink plenty of fluids, check more often, and contact your doctor early. Seek urgent care for repeated vomiting, trouble breathing, confusion or severe drowsiness.

Looking after your feet

Diabetes can damage nerves and reduce blood flow, so injuries may go unnoticed. Look at your feet every day for cuts, blisters, sore spots or colour changes. Wash and dry them well, wear shoes that fit, and avoid walking barefoot. Tell your doctor promptly about any wound that is slow to heal.

Regular check-ups

Complications of diabetes often develop silently, so attend scheduled eye, kidney and foot checks even when you feel well. Your doctor will also order blood tests that show your average blood sugar over recent months. Read our article on diabetes complications and why check-ups matter for more detail.

Taking medicine consistently

Take diabetes medicine exactly as prescribed and do not stop because your numbers look good. They look good because the treatment is working. If side effects or cost are a problem, speak to your doctor or pharmacist rather than skipping doses.

Looking after your mind

Living with a long-term condition can be tiring and stressful. Talk about it with family, friends or your healthcare team. Having one trusted person who understands your routine can make the daily effort easier to sustain.

Small, repeated habits do the most good. Pick one change to start this week, build it into your day, and add the next when it feels natural.

Key takeaways

  • Regular meals, daily walking and consistent checks matter more than perfection.
  • Learn the signs of low blood sugar and keep a fast-acting sugar source close.
  • Check your feet daily and keep eye, kidney and foot appointments.

Quick FAQ

How often should I check my blood sugar?

It depends on your treatment and your doctor's advice. Ask what times are right for you and record every result.
