Malaria: Symptoms, When to Go to Hospital, and Prevention

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team3 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

Malaria is spread by the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It remains a major cause of illness and death in Nigeria, especially among young children and pregnant women. It is also preventable and treatable, so knowing the facts can protect you and your family.

Symptoms

Symptoms usually begin about one to two weeks after an infectious bite, though this varies. Typical signs are fever, chills and sweating, headache, body aches, tiredness, nausea and vomiting. In children, malaria may show as fever, poor feeding, vomiting or unusual irritability.

These symptoms resemble many other illnesses, including typhoid and viral infections. Because of this, guessing is risky. A person can be treated for the wrong illness while the real one gets worse.

Get tested before treating

A rapid diagnostic test or a blood smear at a clinic, health centre or pharmacy laboratory can confirm malaria within minutes to hours. The right medicine depends on the result. If you have a fever, ask for a test instead of buying tablets on a hunch. Children with fever should be seen the same day.

Warning signs of severe malaria

Go to a hospital urgently if there is any of the following:

  • Confusion, unusual sleepiness or difficulty waking.
  • Convulsions or fits.
  • Difficulty breathing.
  • Yellow eyes or skin, or very dark urine.
  • Severe weakness, or being unable to sit or stand.
  • Repeated vomiting so that medicines will not stay down.
  • Passing little or no urine.

Severe malaria can become life-threatening within hours, so do not wait.

Who is at higher risk

Young children, pregnant women, older adults and people with weakened immunity are more likely to become seriously ill. Pregnant women with malaria are at risk of anaemia, miscarriage and low birth weight. Anyone in these groups with a fever should seek care promptly.

Treatment

Uncomplicated malaria is usually treated with artemisinin-based combination medicines, which is the standard recommended by the World Health Organization. Your doctor, nurse or pharmacist will choose the right medicine and dose. Always finish the full course, even if you feel better after a day or two, because stopping early can let the infection return. Do not use leftover tablets from a previous illness.

Prevention that works

No single step is enough, but together they greatly reduce the risk:

  • Sleep under an insecticide-treated net every night. Hang it properly, tuck it in, and repair or replace nets with holes.
  • Remove breeding sites. Mosquitoes lay eggs in standing water. Clear puddles, empty containers, tyres and blocked gutters, and cover water storage.
  • Screen your home. Fit window and door screens where possible, and close doors at dusk.
  • Use repellent in the evening and wear long sleeves when mosquitoes are most active, from dusk to dawn.
  • Attend antenatal care if pregnant. Preventive treatment against malaria is offered during pregnancy.

Common myths

Malaria does not come from the sun, stress, hard work or eating oily food. It is caused by a parasite carried by mosquitoes. Believing otherwise can delay the testing and treatment that make the difference.

Looking after someone with malaria

Encourage fluids and rest, give medicines exactly as prescribed, and watch closely for any warning signs above. If the person is not improving within a day or two of treatment, or is getting worse, return to the clinic. For more on what to do in urgent situations, read our guide to home first aid basics.

The key points are simple: test before you treat, finish your medicine, protect yourself with nets and clean surroundings, and never delay care when warning signs appear.

Key takeaways

  • Fever, chills, headache and body aches are common, but only a test confirms malaria.
  • Confusion, convulsions, breathing difficulty and yellow eyes need urgent hospital care.
  • Sleep under a treated net and clear stagnant water around your home.

Quick FAQ

Should I treat a fever as malaria without a test?

Testing first is safer, because other illnesses cause similar symptoms and need different treatment.
