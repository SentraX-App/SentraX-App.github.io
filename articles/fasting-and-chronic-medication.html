Fasting and Chronic Medication: Talk to Your Doctor Before You Start

By the Sentra-X Forte Limited editorial team3 min readUpdated 15 November 2026

Many people fast for religious reasons during Ramadan, Lent or at other times of the year. If you take daily medicine for high blood pressure, diabetes, heart disease or another long-term condition, fasting can affect both your health and your treatment. The timing and dose of medicines may need to change, and some people are advised against fasting. This article explains what to discuss with your doctor and how to stay safe. It is general information, not personal advice.

Plan before the fast begins

Speak with your doctor or pharmacist well before the fast starts, not on the first day. Bring a list of all your medicines, and ask for a clear plan in writing. Many faith traditions allow exemptions for people whose health could be harmed, and a doctor can help you decide in a way that respects both your health and your faith. You may also wish to speak to your religious leader.

Who needs extra care

Take particular care, and get advice first, if you:

  • Use insulin or certain diabetes tablets.
  • Have heart failure, kidney disease or recent illness or hospital stay.
  • Are pregnant, breastfeeding or elderly and frail.
  • Have blood pressure that has been hard to control.

Diabetes and fasting

People using insulin or some diabetes tablets face a higher risk of dangerously low blood sugar when meals are skipped. Warning signs include shaking, sweating, confusion, hunger and a fast heartbeat. If these occur, break the fast and treat the low blood sugar. High blood sugar is also a risk, especially after large sweet meals. Check your sugar more often than usual, and ask your doctor at which readings you should break the fast. Read more in our guide to a daily diabetes routine.

Blood pressure and fasting

Blood pressure tablets may need to be moved to the pre-dawn or evening meal, or the dose adjusted. Medicines that remove fluid can cause dehydration and low blood pressure when you cannot drink during the day. Signs of low blood pressure include dizziness, fainting and weakness. Check your readings more often, write them down, and show them to your doctor. See how to measure at home.

Do not change doses on your own

Never stop, halve or double a dose without advice. Do not skip your medicine because you are fasting, and do not take extra to make up for it. Ask your doctor exactly when to take each tablet.

Eating and drinking well outside fasting hours

  • Drink plenty of water between sunset and dawn.
  • At the pre-dawn meal, choose slow-release foods such as beans, whole grains and vegetables with some protein, and go easy on salt.
  • When breaking the fast, start gently and avoid very salty, very sugary or very fatty meals. See salt and blood pressure.
  • Limit tea, coffee and cola, which can make you pass more fluid.

When to break the fast

Break the fast and seek help if you feel shaky, sweaty, confused, faint, very weak, have chest pain, or find breathing hard. Your doctor will tell you what readings should make you stop. Safety comes first, and breaking a fast for health reasons is allowed in most traditions.

Afterwards

When the fast ends, return to your usual medicine schedule as advised, and book a review if your readings changed. Taking medicine on time, as in our adherence guide, matters even more during times of change.

Key takeaways

  • Speak with your doctor well before the fast begins.
  • Never stop or halve a dose on your own.
  • Break the fast if you feel shaky, sweaty or confused.

Quick FAQ

Is it safe to fast if I take diabetes medicine?

Some people can with adjusted doses, and others are advised not to. Your doctor will guide you.
